    Diagnosis Out

    A hospital taught a machine to decide which patients needed calling back for an urgent blood test. The code was correct. The arithmetic was correct. The machine was confident. It was also frightening perfectly healthy people.

    Nobody had made a programming mistake. In the next twenty minutes you will find out what did go wrong — by auditing the twelve records the machine learned from, repairing them, and watching a real diagnosis change.

    Four patients, waiting for an answer. The dashed line is the machine's cut-off: stand to the right of it and you get called back for testing. Watch what happens to Ayesha and Daniel when somebody cleans the data.

    Eight stops. Nothing to calculate — the machine does the arithmetic, you make the judgements.

    Stop 1 The callbacks

    A machine that was very confident

    At Meridian General Hospital, a screening model reads one number from a patient's yearly check-up — their fasting blood sugar — and decides one thing: should this person be called back for a full diabetes test, yes or no?

    For the first month it ran quietly in the background. Then the nurses noticed something. The model was calling back far more people than anyone expected, and almost every one of them came back clear.

    Each of those callbacks is a real person. A letter arriving that says the hospital would like to see you again. A week of worrying. A morning taken off school or work. A needle in your arm. For nothing.

    The hospital brought in an auditor to find the bug in the code.

    There was no bug in the code.

    The machine was not broken. It learned exactly what it was shown. It was shown something wrong.

    Your job

    You are the auditor. The hospital has handed you the twelve patient records the model learned from. Somewhere in that small table is the reason healthy people are being frightened. Find it, repair it, and prove what difference it makes.

    Stop 2 The line

    How the model draws its line

    Before you can find the fault, you need to know exactly what the model does with the data. It is simpler than you might expect.

    The model is handed past patients, each with a blood sugar reading and a label saying whether that person turned out to be healthy or diabetic. It then does three things, and only three things.

    The whole algorithm

    One. Work out the average blood sugar of everyone labelled Healthy.

    Two. Work out the average blood sugar of everyone labelled Diabetic.

    Three. Draw the line exactly halfway between those two averages.

    After that, any new patient above the line gets called back for a test. Anyone below the line does not.

    Six past patients. Three healthy, three diabetic. Press the button and follow what the model does.

    Try a new patient

    Drag the slider. The model does not think about this person at all — it only asks which side of the line they are standing on.

    145 mg/dL

    This is a real algorithm

    Computer scientists call this a nearest centroid classifier. "Centroid" is only a grand word for the average position of a group. The model is asking one question about every new patient: which group's average is this person closer to? Simple as it looks, versions of it are used in real medical and biological research today.

    The line is not written by a human. It is calculated from the data. Change one number in the table and the line moves — and people standing on the wrong side of it are given a different answer about their own health.

    Stop 3 First training

    Feed it the records

    These are the twelve records the hospital handed you, exactly as the model received them. Fasting blood sugar is measured in milligrams per decilitre, written mg/dL. For a healthy adult it usually sits somewhere between 70 and 99.

    Load them into the machine and watch it work out what it believes.

    Look at that first number again

    The model believes the average healthy patient has a blood sugar of 56. No such person exists. Somebody reading 56 would be on the floor, not filling in a form.

    It was never comparing new patients against healthy people at all. It was comparing them against a phantom, assembled out of broken cells in the table.

    Blue figures are the healthy patients who were recorded properly. The grey figure is the only "healthy person" this model has ever imagined — assembled out of a zero and a five that were never real readings. Everybody to the right of the red line gets a letter.

    Five cells in that table are worth stopping on. Four are faults. One is not.

    Stop 4 The audit

    Trust nothing

    Read every single cell. Click anything you would not be willing to bet a patient's health on. You have deliberately not been told what to look for — this is what a real audit feels like.

    Before you start

    A number can be neatly typed, correctly spelled, sensibly formatted, sitting in the right column, and still be completely wrong. Nothing in this table is smudged or obviously broken. That is the point.

    Every cell is clickable. Wrong guesses cost you nothing — auditors check things that turn out to be fine all day long.

    Four kinds of fault hide in tables like this one:

    A number that no living body could produce. A number measured in the wrong units. The same person written down twice. A label the computer cannot read.

    And one number that looks alarming but is telling the truth.

    Fix, fill, or drop

    Fix — you can recover the true value with certainty. Nothing is invented, nothing is lost.

    Fill — you cannot recover it, so you estimate it, usually with the average. Honest and common, but never forget what you have done: you have put a guess into a table of measurements, and from that moment on it looks exactly like evidence.

    Drop — the value is gone, no honest estimate exists, or the row is a copy of another. You lose a patient from the study, which hurts. Keeping a lie hurts more.

    Field notes

    Audit complete

    Two numbers repaired, one label corrected, two rows removed — and one very strange reading left exactly where it was, because it was true.

    An outlier only ever tells you that a value is unusual. It never tells you the value is wrong. Working out which one you are looking at is the whole job.

    Stop 5 Second training

    The repaired records

    The same study after your audit. Ten rows instead of twelve, two numbers repaired, one label corrected. Nothing has been invented and nothing true has been thrown away.

    Load them into exactly the same machine. Not one line of its code has changed.

    Same code, different beliefs

    The average healthy patient now reads 91 mg/dL — a number a real person could actually have — and the line has moved 19 points up the scale, from 113 to 132.

    The dirty records dragged the line down to 113. Cleaning put it back at 132. Everybody standing in that shaded gap was told something different about their health depending on which model happened to be running.

    Stop 7 What did we learn

    Three questions

    There is no single right wording for any of these. Answer out loud, or in your notebook, then open the box and see whether an auditor would agree with you.

    Question one

    P09's reading of 216 was far stranger than P04's reading of 5.0 — and yet one of them was kept and the other was repaired. How do you decide whether a surprising number is an error or the truth?

    You check whether the number agrees with everything else you know about that person. P09 is labelled diabetic, and 216 is entirely possible for untreated diabetes — the number and the label tell the same story, so it stays. P04 is labelled healthy, and 5.0 is impossible for any living person in mg/dL — but perfectly ordinary in mmol/L, which is exactly what a partner laboratory would have used. The story only makes sense one way. Strangeness is a reason to investigate, never a reason to delete.

    Question two

    P03's cell was never blank. It held an ordinary looking number, and that is exactly what made it dangerous. Why does missing data disguised as a real value do more damage than an empty cell would?

    An empty cell announces itself. Every tool you own will stop, complain, or skip it, and a human will be asked what to do. A zero says nothing — it slides into the arithmetic like any other measurement and drags the average down with it, silently. The model never gets the chance to know that it is missing something, so nobody gets the chance to fix it.

    Question three

    Here the dirty model raised false alarms — it frightened healthy people. Describe a way dirty data could cause the opposite failure, a model that misses genuinely ill patients. Then say which of the two a hospital should fear more, and defend it.

    Flip the faults. If a healthy patient's reading had been recorded as 900 instead of 90, or a diabetic patient's 155 had been entered as 15, the healthy average would shoot up, the line would climb far above 132, and genuinely ill people would be told they are fine. Which is worse is a real argument with no settled answer: false alarms waste money, clinic time and cause real fear, and enough of them teach staff to ignore the model altogether. Missed cases cost years of somebody's health. Most screening programmes deliberately accept more false alarms to avoid missed cases — but that is a choice a hospital makes on purpose, not one a broken spreadsheet should make for it.

    Did you know?

    In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter, a spacecraft that had travelled for nine months and cost around 125 million dollars. It did not crash because of a broken engine or a coding error. One team's software worked in imperial units of force while the receiving system expected metric ones. Nobody converted between them. The numbers looked perfectly reasonable to everybody involved, and the spacecraft flew far too close to Mars and was destroyed.

    A unit that nobody wrote down is one of the most expensive mistakes in the history of space flight. P04's row is exactly the same mistake, in a hospital.

    And this really happens

    One of the most widely used diabetes datasets in the world, studied by machine learning students everywhere, contains patients whose recorded blood pressure is zero, whose skin thickness is zero, and whose body mass index is zero. None of those are possible in a living person. Every one of them is a missing measurement written down as a number, exactly like P03's row.

    Students who train a model on that data without noticing get a working model, a confident model, and a wrong one. Students who check first do not. The only difference between them is whether somebody looked.

    A note on real medicine

    Real clinics do not let a model invent its own cut-off. They use figures agreed by international medical bodies after decades of research. The model in this tour learns its line from its own data instead, which is precisely why that data has to be trustworthy. And if you ever have a question about your own health, the person to ask is a doctor, never a spreadsheet.

    A model can only ever be as good as the data it was shown. Cleaning is not tidying up before the real work begins. It is the real work.
